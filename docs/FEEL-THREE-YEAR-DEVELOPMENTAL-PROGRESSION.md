# FEEL — Three-Year Developmental Progression

**Status:** Approved Working Framework — 2026-09-12

## Developmental Spine

> **NOTICE → NAME → REGULATE WITH SUPPORT → INTERACT → CARE → BEGIN TO CHOOSE**

This is a conceptual curriculum reference, not a rigid age gate or formal assessment.

## Three-Year Arc

### Year 1 — Recognize & Feel Safe
**NOTICE → NAME → REGULATE WITH SUPPORT**

Build foundations: notice emotional cues, connect common situations with simple emotions, experience predictable co-regulation, and learn that adults/characters can help. Children may respond through gesture, action, home language, English, mixed language, or silence.

### Year 2 — Practice Together
**REGULATE WITH SUPPORT → INTERACT → CARE**

Use play and routines to practice waiting, turn-taking, joining play, asking for help, handling frustration with support, noticing another person's emotion, and offering simple help.

### Year 3 — Choose, Repair & Collaborate
**INTERACT → CARE → BEGIN TO CHOOSE**

Increase agency: choose among safe responses, explain simple feelings/needs, participate in conflict repair, consider another person's needs, adapt when plans change, and collaborate toward shared outcomes.

## Area Progression

| Area | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emotional recognition | notice familiar cues | name/connect emotion to situation | describe/compare feelings in context |
| Regulation | co-regulate with adult | try supported strategies | choose/adapt safe strategies with support |
| Social interaction | parallel/early turn-taking | short cooperative exchanges | sustained cooperation and simple negotiation |
| Empathy | notice another's emotion | care/help in concrete situations | consider needs and possible perspectives |
| Self-awareness | preferences, needs, boundaries | communicate preferences/needs | reflect on choices and adapt |
| Frustration/flexibility | recover with support | retry/change strategy | handle variation and simple setbacks with increasing agency |
| Repair | adult-supported repair | participate in repair | initiate simple repair when appropriate |

## Complexity Model

Progress should increase across four dimensions:

- **Emotional complexity:** visible/familiar → mixed/contextual → more nuanced situations
- **Regulation complexity:** adult-led co-regulation → shared strategy → child begins choosing
- **Social complexity:** parallel/one turn → short exchange → cooperative problem solving
- **Agency:** observe → participate → choose → initiate → adapt

Progress is not measured by how calm, obedient, or emotionally articulate a child appears.

## Spiral Examples

### Waiting for a turn
- Year 1: notice “wait” and tolerate a short supported pause.
- Year 2: wait and use a simple phrase such as “my turn, please.”
- Year 3: negotiate order/time with another child and adapt when the plan changes.

### Anger/frustration
- Year 1: recognize angry/frustrated and accept adult support.
- Year 2: name feeling and try a supported response such as pause/help/move away.
- Year 3: describe the problem, choose a safe response, and participate in finding a solution.

### Friend is sad
- Year 1: notice sad face/crying.
- Year 2: identify “sad” and offer simple help.
- Year 3: ask/check in, consider what may help, and adapt response.

### Conflict over a toy
- Year 1: recognize wanting/being upset and accept adult support.
- Year 2: ask, wait, take turns, and use simple social language.
- Year 3: communicate needs, consider another child's turn, negotiate, and repair when needed.

## What Progress Does NOT Mean

- memorizing more emotion words;
- never becoming upset;
- independent self-regulation at all times;
- always sharing;
- always saying sorry;
- always choosing the “nice” response;
- suppressing anger, fear, sadness, or excitement;
- speaking English about emotions;
- correctly identifying another person's inner state every time.

## Design Implication

FEEL episodes should revisit familiar emotional/social functions repeatedly while increasing context, agency, interaction, and flexibility. The curriculum should remain play-based and responsive rather than becoming a behavior-training program.
